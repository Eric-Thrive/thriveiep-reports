How to build questions that earn their claims

A psychometric evidence guide for developing role-aware observer questions and narrowly targeted student companion questions for C2A’s working adult learners.

Executive answer

Start with an observable behavior, a named context, and a modest claim.

The research does not support a generic observer score or a quick conversion of student self-report into third-person wording. It supports a staged development path: define one role-and-setting-specific behavior family; test whether raters understand and can actually observe it; then establish what the resulting evidence can responsibly support.

Recommended first pass: one setting, one rater role, 3–5 original behavioral prototypes, a four-week direct-observation window, and a structural No opportunity to observe option. Pair it, if useful, with one student task-confidence question tied to a separately verifiable outcome. Do cognitive interviews and rater orientation before calculating any score.
Observer channel

Visible behavior in context

Ask a direct observer what they saw during a defined activity. Keep the claim to that role, setting, and time window.

Student channel

Private experience or confidence

Ask the student about a named upcoming task, reason, or experience; do not infer it from behavior or an observer.

Not yet

Scores, flags, or change claims

Norms, pooled roles, discrepancy flags, and progress monitoring each require evidence beyond an initial feasibility study.

Development workflow

Build evidence in the order it can actually support a claim.

  1. Write the intended claim first. For each observer item, record behavior, task/setting, eligible role, minimum exposure, recall window, allowed interpretation, forbidden interpretation, and intended coaching use.
  2. Elicit concrete episodes. Interview target learners and eligible observers separately. Ask what happened, which cues were used, where it would be invisible, and what wording would be ambiguous.
  3. Draft and orient. Make 3–5 original prototypes. Test a brief explanation of what counts, what does not, and when to use no opportunity; use a practice vignette if it helps.
  4. Test response process and feasibility. Use cognitive interviews. Track burden, no-opportunity rate, missingness, floor/ceiling concentration, and guessing risk. Do not compute a total score.
  5. Test the next claim only when ready. Structure, stability, role/source variance, and relations to an independent criterion depend on the intended use. Longitudinal responsiveness is a separate study.
Illustrative item brief: “During shared work you directly observed in the past four weeks, the learner updated the next step after a planned task was blocked.” This may support a conversation about a visible blocker-response pattern. It cannot support a conclusion about global resilience, motivation, diagnosis, or improvement over time.
Advancement gates

Stop before a claim outruns the evidence.

GateMinimum evidenceStop condition
G0 · Construct and rightsConstruct brief, intended use, original/licensed content record.Private-state claim or unclear content rights.
G1 · ObservabilityEligible role, setting, direct opportunity, no-opportunity route.Raters cannot cite direct observation.
G2 · Response processCognitive interviews and rater-orientation evidence.Raters infer motive, trait, or diagnosis; window/anchors are interpreted inconsistently.
G3 · FeasibilityBurden, distribution, missingness, and role-separated pilot results.High guessing/no-opportunity or little usable variation.
G4–G5 · Measurement and meaningUse-appropriate structure/reliability plus a pre-specified independent criterion.A pooled composite is less defensible than item-level evidence, or only same-method agreement exists.
G6 · Change and report useLongitudinal evidence, fairness/consequence review, and report-language controls.Team wants individual progress, discrepancy, or automated report claims without this evidence.
Final recommendation: treat the first C2A study as an observability and response-process study, not a scale launch. Advance one role-specific behavior family only after raters can use it consistently and it has a separate, predeclared practical criterion. Keep private constructs in student companion methods, with task-specific confidence as the most practical early pilot.
